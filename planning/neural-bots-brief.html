Technical brief · reinforcement learning

Teaching a bot to move fast through an arena shooter

A PPO policy that replaces hand-written movement code in a Quake-lineage arena FPS. It runs inside the real game simulation, sees the world through a precomputed geometry lattice rather than pixels or raycasts, and is scored on how quickly it gets from A to B.

What I'd like advice on. The system works, but most of the hard-won progress came from finding defects in the objective and the data rather than from tuning. The last section lists what broke — it is the part worth your scepticism, and the shape of those failures explains most of the design choices above it.

The problem

Get from a start point to a target as fast as possible, on maps the policy has never seen.

Classic bots in this genre navigate a hand-authored waypoint graph and move with scripted rules. They are reliable and slow — they don't bunnyhop, don't chain strafe jumps, and don't use weapons for movement. A skilled human does all three and is roughly twice as fast across a map.

The policy owns movement only. Deterministic game code still decides where to go and handles combat targeting; the policy decides how to get there. That split matters for the design: the policy has to accept a goal that can change mid-route, and it has to hand back control of the crosshair when the combat code needs to shoot something.

Hard constraints

  • No rendering, no per-frame raycasting. Sixteen bots share a server tick. The whole perception budget is a few microseconds per bot per decision.
  • No waypoint data as a policy input. The waypoint graph is used as training scaffolding to pick start/target pairs, but it is never fed to the network — otherwise the policy learns to follow a graph rather than to read geometry.
  • Must generalise to unseen maps. Three maps are held out of training entirely and refused at load, so the generalisation number can't be quietly contaminated.

Setup

The environment is the actual game, run headless.

The engine's simulation, collision, weapon and trigger code are all portable and have no rendering dependency, so the trainer runs the real game loop rather than a reimplementation. That costs about 30 microseconds per sample — five orders of magnitude more than a toy environment — and every batch-size decision follows from that.

The environment is C#; the trainer is Python and PyTorch. They talk over a local socket: observations out, actions back, one round trip per decision step. Worlds run concurrently and are stepped in lockstep.

Design consequence

Geometry is per world — one process holds one map — but every agent on it draws its own route (a start/target pair plus a Dijkstra flood, a few milliseconds each). Distinct geometries per gradient batch therefore equal the world count, and distinct routes the agent count. Both matter: going from 6 to 24 distinct layouts per batch took a stage from 46% to 95% arrivals before anything else changed, which makes world count the single most important "hyperparameter" in the system — and it does not look like one.

Network

Small on purpose — it has to run for every bot on a live server.

Only the actor ships — 228 KB in fp32, small enough to sit in L2 and be shared by every bot on the server. The critic is discarded after training. The trunks are deliberately not shared: sharing couples the value loss to the policy gradient, which in early runs produced a converged value function and a policy that stopped moving.

Width and depth are fixed, not searched. They were chosen from the per-frame CPU budget rather than from what trains best, which is an obvious place to ask whether we're leaving accuracy on the table.

How the policy sees the world

A precomputed geometry lattice, sampled in a polar pattern around the bot.

This is the part I'd most like scrutinised. Instead of rendering or casting rays each frame, every map is baked once into a navigation field: a 32-unit horizontal grid where each column stores the walkable floor spans above it. Each span is 12 bytes — floor height, ceiling height, surface slope, a content flag (lava, water, void), and a bitmask of which neighbouring cells are reachable by jumping.

Perception is then an array lookup, not a trace. The bot samples the field at three radii × eight compass directions, taking four values at each of the 24 sample points: floor height relative to the bot, headroom, a hazard flag, and the geodesic distance-to-goal delta at that probe versus the bot's own — which directions make progress through the geometry, so walls are implicit. That is the 96-float navfield block below.

Seeing up, and seeing ahead

Two further blocks come from the same auto-generated structures. The floor probes collapse each grid column to the bot's own storey, so an overhead ring re-samples the outer two radii for the nearest walkable surface above the bot — mezzanines, walkways, rocket-jumpable ledges — which were previously invisible despite being stored in the field. And a route ribbon reports six points along the geodesic at 200-unit spacing (egocentric offset, relative height, hazard): "the route climbs 128 units, turns hard left, then crosses a gap", a full second before arriving there.

The ribbon and the probe deltas are the system's map knowledge, and the constraint on them is the design: both derive from a distance transform computed for any map at load. No authored navigation data is ever an input, so nothing degrades on maps that ship without it.

Three things the baked field cannot know — doors mid-swing, other players, moving platforms — are covered by six short box sweeps, the only actual traces in the observation.

Same router in training and live

The distance field is also the runtime router. Live bots used to take their course from the game's classic waypoint route while training took it from the field's Dijkstra flood — a train/deploy distribution gap, and a hard dependency on hand-authored data. The flood is now built per goal on a worker thread (a few milliseconds, goals change every ~6 s), so trained and deployed bots read the same signals from the same source on any map.

Frame choice

Everything egocentric is anchored on the direction of travel, not the camera. The view can be yanked anywhere by combat; if perception rotated with it, every observation during a fight would come from a distribution the policy had barely trained on. Anchoring on the goal direction keeps "ledge on my left" meaning one thing regardless of where the crosshair points.

Six discrete heads and two continuous, sampled independently.

The three weapons are the movement toolkit: a blaster shot at the floor mid-jump gives a large vertical boost, the crylink's secondary gives a forward push while bunnyhopping, and the rocket launcher can be fired downward and detonated to save a fall or cross a wide gap.

Masking, not penalising

When the deterministic combat code needs the weapon, or when the bot's health drops below a threshold, the attack and weapon-select heads are masked off entirely — the action is unavailable, not merely expensive. A reward penalty would give a bot that mostly complies, and combat code can't build on "mostly". The permit is also a policy input, and it is flipped randomly mid-episode during training so the policy learns to handle losing the weapon at any moment.

Reward

Dense progress shaping, a time cost, and terminal outcomes.

TermWeightNotes
Geodesic progress+0.01 / unitDistance closed along the actual route, not straight-line. ≈ +0.22/step at running speed
Time−0.02 / stepFlat. ≈ −18 over a full episode

Turn / jerk−0.004 × turnBuys smooth motion instead of twitching
Arrival+10 + 60 × budget left+63 at step 100, +17 at step 800
Death / fell out of world−20
Timeout0Truncation, not termination — the value function still bootstraps

Progress uses the plain difference d − d′ rather than the discounted potential form γφ(s′) − φ(s). The discounted form is what the shaping theorem is stated in, and it pays a stationary agent d(1−γ) per step: at 1000 units from the target that was +0.1 per step for standing still, and random actions scored a positive mean reward. The plain difference is zero for a stationary agent and telescopes to exactly the distance closed.

The interaction that bit us

Note the arithmetic between the time cost and the death penalty. Surviving a full episode accrues about −18 from the time term alone. The death penalty was originally −5. Dying at step 100 therefore cost about −5.7 against −6.3 for surviving to the cap — suicide was strictly cheaper than finishing, and the policy found it: 65% of episodes ended in deliberate death against 6.5% for a scripted straight-line baseline, and the objective scored the two strategies within 0.08 of each other.

This also means the obvious lever for "make it faster" — a bigger time penalty — is the one to avoid. Speed pressure lives in the arrival bonus instead, because a bonus paid only on success cannot make any failure more attractive.

Curriculum

Six stages, ramping on route length and map complexity.

Five of the six stages run on the game's real shipped maps. Start and target positions are drawn from the map's own waypoint nodes (or, on maps without a waypoint graph, from spawn points and item pickups), then verified reachable: a Dijkstra flood over the baked navigation field from the target, rejecting any pair with no walkable path or a route shorter than the stage's minimum.

  1. 01

    Full map pool, 700–1200 unit routes

    Run and turn on real geometry. Gate 80% against a 50.9% scripted baseline.

  2. 02

    Full map pool, 1200–2500

    Long enough that the only route to a good time is building and holding speed — which is what makes bunnyhopping reward-maximising rather than a trick we have to demonstrate. Gate 60% / 28.4%.

  3. 03

    Full map pool, 700–1500

    Stairwells, tight doorways, railings, multi-level loops. Gate 60% / 29.7%.

  4. 04

    Full map pool, 1500–3000

    Gate 50% / 22.9%.

  5. 05

    Generated weapon-jump courses

    The only synthetic stage. It builds a 560-unit gap against a ~320-unit running jump, and a 250-unit ledge against a ~105-unit jump apex, so a rocket or blaster jump is the only way through. A real map's route almost always has a walkable path, so a policy trained only on real maps is never forced to weapon-jump and would likely never discover it. Gate 45%.

  6. 06

    Full map pool, uncapped route length

    The real distribution, including routes spanning most of an arena. Gate 55% / 37.0%.

Every gate is set against a measured baseline: a scripted "run straight at the target, hop rhythmically" policy on that stage's own maps and route band, at roughly double the baseline. The point of measuring first is that the gate then makes a claim about the policy rather than about whether a run happens to be passing it.

Training loop

Standard PPO, with batch sizes driven by how expensive a sample is.

  1. Each of 20 worlds loads a map and spawns 16 agents, each drawing its own verified route.
  2. Every agent builds its 302-float observation after the step's physics, at the step boundary.
  3. The trainer normalises, runs the actor, and samples an action from the six discrete heads and two Gaussians.
  4. Actions go back; each world advances 4 simulation ticks and returns rewards, done and truncated flags.
  5. After 128 steps, compute GAE and run 4 epochs × 4 minibatches of clipped PPO, with a KL early-stop.
  6. Every 25 updates, export the weights and evaluate them through the shipping code path — the network running inside the game's own bot locomotor, with sampling off.
  7. When that evaluation clears the stage gate, advance.

Two evolutionary layers

Both are bounded, both are scored by the same deterministic bench as the gate, and both exist because of a measured failure mode rather than on principle.

  • Stage-entry learning-rate tournament. Each stage's reward scale differs, so at stage entry three branches probe ~500k steps from one snapshot at lr ×0.5 / ×1 / ×2; the winner continues with its lr. Automates a selection we kept making by hand.
  • Plateau perturb-and-select. If the shipped eval makes no new best for four consecutive evaluations, one GA generation fires: six noise-perturbed copies of the weights (σ = 2–10% of each tensor's scale), each scored through the bench, adopted only on a +0.5 pp win, at most three rounds per stage. Its first act is re-measuring the scripted baseline — in this project's history every plateau was a task defect rather than an optimiser trap, and weight noise cannot fix a mis-specified task.

Evaluation detail worth copying

Training samples actions; the evaluation takes the argmax. Those score very differently, and the gap is diagnostic rather than noise — a near-uniform distribution can still have an informative argmax. We gate on the deterministic shipping path because that's what a live server runs; gating on the sampled rate would gate on a number the shipped bot never experiences.

What actually went wrong

Nearly all real progress came from finding defects, not from tuning.

Listing these because they're the most transferable part, and because the pattern in them is the thing I'd like a second opinion on. A long plateau was attacked with entropy coefficient, discount factor, learning rate, action-space framing and exploration noise — five hyperparameters, each of which moved its own diagnostic and none of which moved the metric. That pattern was itself the signal, and it took far too long to read it as "the objective or the data is wrong, not the optimiser".

DefectSymptomEffect of fix
Dying cheaper than surviving 65% of episodes ended in deliberate death; the objective ranked that equal to a strategy arriving 2.4× more often Deaths → 0% on early stages; returns turned positive
Too few distinct courses per batch 6 layouts per gradient update; policy overfit to them and the held-out evaluation stalled at 46% 24 layouts → 95%, and it was also the fastest configuration
Early stages restricted to four "simple" maps Distinct geometries per batch fell to 4; the policy plateaued at 52–56% for 20M steps — below the straight-line baseline's 59.9% Full pool at every stage; difficulty carried by route length alone
Finished agents padded the batch An agent that finished kept emitting zero-observation, zero-reward rows until its whole world reset — up to 83% of the buffer, fed straight into the observation normaliser whose statistics ship in the weight file Corrected; didn't move arrivals but was wrong on its own terms
Exploration noise ran away The Gaussian log-std drifted to σ = 0.91 of range — 20°/step of yaw, ~360°/s of random spin. Differential entropy is unbounded above, so the entropy bonus kept buying more Capped; sampled behaviour became coherent
Clamped action, unclamped log-prob Stored the post-clamp action against the pre-clamp log-prob, so PPO's importance ratio was wrong on the first epoch when it should be exactly 1. Symptom was a negative KL estimate Unblocked learning entirely

Methodology lesson

Three separate times a knob under test turned out not to be connected — a config setter that silently no-opped before initialisation, a checkpoint restore that overwrote the learning rate being swept, a flag latched into a static before it was set. Each produced results indistinguishable from a cleanly rejected hypothesis. Every tunable now echoes its resolved value at startup, and it's the cheapest reliability improvement in the project.
